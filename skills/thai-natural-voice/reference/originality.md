# Originality and attribution

Use public writing as evidence of communication patterns, not as a phrase bank.

## Named-style requests

When the user asks for a living creator, influencer, publication, or brand by name:

1. Resolve a bundled publication alias to its canonical `-inspired` preset; otherwise extract 3–5 non-identifying traits at the level of register, pacing, structure, density, and reader relationship.
2. Apply a known broad preset silently. State the generalized direction only for exact-imitation, official-authorship, endorsement, or unknown-source requests.
3. Write original text using the selected broad archetype.
4. Remove signature phrases, slogans, branded section names, repeated catchphrases, and distinctive narrative sequences.
5. Avoid labeling the result as written by, endorsed by, or officially belonging to the named source.

For a supplied passage, preserve its facts and purpose while changing its wording and structure. Quote only the amount the user's task genuinely requires.

## Identity and endorsement

Write as the user's disclosed role or organization only when the user has established that context. Do not invent first-hand experiences, access, quotes, readership size, professional credentials, or institutional authority.

For news and public-interest content:

- Attribute claims to their actual source.
- Separate confirmed fact, allegation, estimate, and opinion.
- Preserve uncertainty and timestamps.
- Avoid sensational language that raises certainty or urgency beyond the evidence.

## Open-source hygiene

Keep the distributable skill clean:

- Store observations and links, not scraped corpora.
- Use original examples written for the skill.
- Describe patterns as archetypes, not as replicas of named voices.
- Keep source names inside input aliases and research provenance—not output profile names, examples, bylines, sign-offs, or hashtags.
- Treat logos, screenshots, post text, and brand assets as excluded unless separately licensed.

These constraints reduce copyright, trademark, passing-off, and impersonation risk; they are design guardrails, not legal advice.
